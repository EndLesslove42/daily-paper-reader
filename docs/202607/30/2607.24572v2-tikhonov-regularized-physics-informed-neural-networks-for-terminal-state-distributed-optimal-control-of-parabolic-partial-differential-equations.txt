# Tikhonov-Regularized Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Terminal-State Distributed Optimal Control of Parabolic Partial Differential Equations 

## Nguyen Thanh Quang ∗1 and Ta Thi Thanh Mai †11Faculty of Mathematics and Informatics, Hanoi University of Science and Technology, Ha Noi, Viet Nam July 29, 2026 

Abstract 

In this study, we propose a Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) framework that incorporates Tikhonov regularization to solve terminal-state tracking optimal control con-strained by parabolic partial differential equations (PDEs). This problem is inherently ill-posed, as infinitely many distributed controls may drive the system to the same de-sired state, so the regularization guides the optimizer toward the minimum-energy control, restoring numerical stability and yielding a smooth, physically meaningful solution. On the theoretical side, we establish a consistency result showing that PINNs minimizers nearly attain the continuous regularized objective under residual and quadrature approximation assumptions, and a novel error estimate that bounds the deviation of the learned control from the minimum-energy solution in terms of the PINNs training residuals and the regu-larization parameter. Numerical experiments on the linear heat equation and the nonlinear Burgers’ equation demonstrate that the regularized PINNs framework accurately achieves the target terminal state while producing controls that have significantly lower energy and smoother profiles than their unregularized counterparts. 

Keywords: Optimal control; Physics-informed neural networks; Tikhonov regularization; Ill-posed problems; Terminal-state tracking. 

# 1 Introduction 

Optimal control of PDE-governed systems plays a central role in engineering and scientific com-puting, with applications in thermal regulation, fluid mechanics, environmental management, and biomedicine [1, 2, 3, 4]. A particularly challenging class is the terminal-state optimal control problem . Its aim is to choose a control distributed over space and time so that the solution of the governing PDE is as close as possible to a prescribed target at the final time. The discrepancy between the resulting terminal state and the target state defines the objective to be minimized, while the PDE, boundary conditions, and initial condition act as constraints [5, 6]. A fundamental difficulty is the inherent ill-posedness of this problem [7, 8]: due to the smoothing properties of parabolic PDEs [9], infinitely many controls may produce the same terminal state, violating Hadamard’s uniqueness condition. Tikhonov regularization is a classical remedy [10, 11]. Among all controls achieving the target, it selects the one with minimum L2 or 

H1 norm, yielding smooth, oscillation-free solutions [12, 13]. Crucially, this penalty is not part of the original terminal-tracking objective but is introduced at the solver level [14], preserving the control objective while restoring coercivity and numerical stability. Classical theory guarantees convergence to the minimum-energy control as α → 0+ [10, 15]. Traditional mesh-based, adjoint-based methods for PDE-constrained control [16] are mathe-matically rigorous but require specialized expertise and become costly in high dimensions or for nonlinear problems. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) [17] offer a mesh-free alterna-tive that embeds PDE residuals directly into the loss function, bypassing explicit mesh generation

PINNs have been applied to forward problems such as elasticity [18], in-verse problems such as Navier-Stokes parameter identification [19], impedance imaging [20], and parabolic inverse source problems [21]. Their application to optimal control has gained consider-able attention [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. However, a systematic integration of Tikhonov regularization within the PINNs loss for terminal-state control, together with principled parameter selection, remains underexplored. In this paper, we propose a Tikhonov-regularized PINNs framework for terminal-state track-ing distributed optimal control of parabolic PDEs. Our contributions are: (i) a PINNs loss incorporating an H1 Tikhonov penalty that guides the optimizer toward minimum-energy solu-tions without modifying the control objective; (ii) a consistency estimate (Proposition 4.1) and, under a standard source condition, a novel error bound (Theorem 4.7) that relates the accuracy of the learned control to the training accuracy and regularization strength; and (iii) numerical validation on the heat and Burgers’ equations using the L-curve criterion [27, 28] for parameter selection. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 presents the precise mathemat-ical formulation of the terminal-state optimal control problem, including the governing parabolic PDE, the terminal-cost functional, and a brief discussion on the inherent ill-posedness caused by the non-uniqueness of distributed controls. Section 3 describes our Tikhonov-regularized Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) framework, in which both the state and the control are approximated by independent neural networks, and the loss function incorporates not only the PDE residuals but also an H1 penalty on the control to enforce smoothness and select the minimum-energy solution. Section 4 provides the theoretical analysis of the proposed method: we first prove a consistency result for the regularized objective when the residuals are small, and then derive a novel error estimate that bounds the L2 deviation of the learned control from the true minimum-energy control in terms of the training residual and the regularization pa-rameter. Section 5 reports numerical experiments on two benchmark problems-the linear heat equation and the nonlinear Burgers’ equation-to illustrate the effectiveness of the regularized PINNs; we compare its performance with that of the corresponding unregularized formulation and also demonstrate the use of the L-curve criterion for selecting the regularization parameter. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper with a summary of our main contributions and a discussion of several promising directions for future work, such as extending the framework to boundary control, adaptive loss-weighting, and rigorous error analysis for fully nonlinear problems.

# 4 Theoretical Analysis: Ill-Posedness, Regularization, and Error Estimates 

In this section, we provide a rigorous mathematical discussion of the terminal-state optimal con-trol problem. We first recall the inherent ill-posedness due to non-uniqueness (Section 4.1). We then analyze the stabilizing effect of the Tikhonov regularization on the control network (Sec-tion 4.2) and establish a quantitative connection to the minimum-energy control (Section 4.3). We next present a consistency result (Proposition 4.1) showing that the PINNs minimizer nearly attains the optimal regularized objective. Finally, we prove a new error estimate (Theorem 4.7) that directly bounds the deviation of the learned control from the minimum-energy solution, using only the residual losses of the PINNs training. 

## 4.1 Ill-Posedness of the Unregularized Problem 

Consider the control-to-state map S : f 7 → uf (·, T ), which assigns to each distributed control 

f ∈ H1(Ω T ) the terminal state of the PDE constraint (1). For linear parabolic equations (e.g., the heat equation), S : H1(Ω T ) → L2(Ω) is a compact linear operator [29]. Compactness alone does not imply non-injectivity. For the distributed-in-time control setting considered here, however, each terminal-state mode depends on a time integral of the corresponding control mode, leaving an infinite-dimensional kernel N (S); hence there are infinitely many non-zero controls fnull with 

S(fnull ) = 0. If f ∗ is any control achieving the desired terminal state ud, then the entire affine set: 

f ∗ + N (S) = {f ∗ + fnull | fnull ∈ N (S)}

also yields the same terminal state and therefore attains the same cost value J(f ∗). Hence the unregularized problem (3) admits infinitely many global minimizers, violating Hadamard’s uniqueness condition. For nonlinear PDEs such as the Burgers’ equation, the control-to-state map is not linear, but the terminal-cost functional remains non-convex and may possess multiple solution branches [8]. Gradient-based optimization applied to such problems often exhibits instability and converges to controls contaminated by high-frequency oscillations, reflecting the underlying non-uniqueness and the lack of coercivity. 

## 4.2 Stabilizing Effect of the Tikhonov Regularization 

Although the original optimal control problem (3) does not contain a regularization term, the PINNs loss function (8) incorporates an explicit H1(Ω T ) Tikhonov penalty αLreg on the control network fη . This modification fundamentally alters the optimization landscape and is essential for obtaining physically meaningful solutions. When F is a linear elliptic operator (e.g., F[u] = −∆u), the reduced terminal-cost functional is convex in the control function f . Adding the squared H1 penalty makes the continuous reduced 4functional strongly convex and coercive on H1(Ω T ); hence, for every fixed α > 0, it admits a unique minimizing control fα. This function-space uniqueness does not imply uniqueness of the raw network parameters: neuron permutations and other parameter symmetries can represent the same pair of functions, and the finite-dimensional PINNs loss remains non-convex in ( θ, η ). Under the assumptions stated in Section 4.3, the continuous minimizers converge strongly in 

H1(Ω T ) to the minimum-energy control as α → 0+.For nonlinear PDEs, the reduced terminal-cost functional is generally non-convex. At the function-space level, the H1 penalty is coercive and weakly lower semicontinuous with respect to the control function f [10]. Together with suitable weak continuity or closedness assumptions on the nonlinear control-to-state map, these properties yield existence of a minimizing control, but not uniqueness. They do not by themselves guarantee existence or uniqueness in the raw neural-network parameter space. In computation, the penalty nevertheless suppresses highly oscillatory control functions and favors smoother local minima.

Remark 4.8 (On the global minimizer assumption) . The hypothesis that PINN training reaches a global minimizer is a standard idealization in theoretical analyses of PINNs [33, 32, 21]. Global-convergence results are available for particular architectures and sufficiently wide-network regimes [34, 35, 36], but they do not automatically apply to the finite networks used here. In our experiments the training loss decays to values of order 10 −5-10 −7 (Figures 2a and 4a), which indicates a low-loss solution but does not by itself certify global optimality. The theorem should therefore be read as conditional on the stated global-minimizer assumption. 

Remark 4.9 (Non-attainable targets) . Theorem 4.7 assumes that the target ud is attainable, so that the minimum-energy control fmin exists. In the numerical experiments of Section 5 the targets are discontinuous step functions, which are not attainable by smooth parabolic flows; consequently fmin does not exist in the classical sense, and the source condition Assumption 4.5 is not strictly satisfied. Nevertheless, the regularized PINNs framework still produces smooth, physically plausible controls. Proposition 4.11 offers a formal local explanation only for the non-linear Burgers case. For the linear heat case, standard regularized least-squares theory explains stability for a non-attainable target, but the convergence estimate to an attainable minimum-energy control stated in Theorem 4.7 does not apply. The experiments therefore demonstrate performance beyond the precise scope of that theorem.

4.5.3 Heuristic estimate for nonlinear problems 

For nonlinear PDEs such as the Burgers’ equation, the control-to-state map S is not linear and the Tikhonov functional (15) is non-convex. Consequently, the strong convexity argument does not apply globally. Nevertheless, if the learned control fη∗ is sufficiently close to the continuous minimizer fα, one can linearize the problem around fα and apply the previous analysis formally. This leads to the following heuristic estimate, which serves as a guideline rather than a rigorous theorem. 

Proposition 4.11 (Nonlinear case, formal estimate) . Let F be a nonlinear operator that is Fr´ echet differentiable with respect to u (e.g. Burgers’ equation, see [38]). Assume that the target

∥fη∗ − fmin ∥L2(Ω T ) ≲ ϵ1/4

√α + α

is expected, with constants depending on the linearization error. Sketch of justification. Let S′(fα) be the Fr´ echet derivative of S at fα. For ∥f − fα∥H1(Ω T )

sufficiently small: 

S(f ) ≈ S (fα) + S′(fα)( f − fα).

Substituting this into Jα yields a locally strongly convex quadratic functional in f −fα. Repeating the steps of Theorem 4.7 within this local region gives the claimed dependence. A rigorous justification would require a quantitative bound on the nonlinear remainder and a proof that fη∗

indeed stays inside the region of convexity; this is beyond the scope of the present work. 

Remark 4.12 . Proposition 4.11 indicates that the error structure O(ϵ1/4/√α + α) is robust, at least in a neighborhood of the optimum. This is consistent with the numerical results for the Burgers’ equation (Section 5), where the regularized PINNs converges to a smooth, physically plausible control even though a rigorous theory is not yet available.

# 6 Conclusion 

We have proposed a Tikhonov-regularized PINNs framework for terminal-state tracking opti-mal control of parabolic PDEs, addressing the inherent ill-posedness from non-uniqueness of optimal controls. On the theoretical side, we established a consistency result (Proposition 4.1) and a novel error estimate (Theorem 4.7); a formal extension to the nonlinear case is given in Proposition 4.11. Numerical experiments on the heat and Burgers’ equations confirm that the regularized framework yields smooth, physically meaningful controls without sacrificing terminal tracking accuracy. 14 Figure 6: Control surfaces fη∗ (x, t ) for the Burgers’ equation: (a) regularized ( α∗ = 5 .62 ×10 −5); (b) unregularized ( α = 0). Future work includes extending the framework to boundary control and more complex sys-tems [39], incorporating adaptive loss-weighting to reduce manual tuning, and developing a rigorous error analysis for the nonlinear case along the lines of [21].
